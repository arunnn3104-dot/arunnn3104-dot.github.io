How do people feel about being interviewed by an AI?

A qualitative study of sixteen people who were interviewed by an AI and then asked what it was like.

Arun N, Thiagarajar School of Business, Madurai. Summer internship report, September 2026. Faculty guide: Dr. Rakesh G Nair.

Safe to speak, not to judge.

People spoke more openly to an AI interviewer than they would to a person, and trusted it to record what they said and find patterns across many people. They did not trust it to decide what their answers meant about them as individuals, unless a named human stayed responsible for the decision.

Do people speak more openly to an AI interviewer?

Yes, on some topics. With no face to read and no reaction to adjust to, several respondents raised things they usually keep to themselves, such as burnout, a suspicion of age-based rejection, or the real reason for a career change. People who normally feel nervous in interviews found it calmer.

"With a person you see them react, a small frown, or they write something down, and you adjust yourself without deciding to. Here there was nothing to read, so I just said the thing."

The openness had limits. Habitual caution, especially about current employers, stayed the same whoever was asking. And the absence that made people feel safe also made the conversation feel flat and one-sided. Several wanted some sign that what they had shared had been heard.

What do people notice about how an AI interviews?

Respondents judged the AI as an interviewer, not just as a questioner.

  • What it did well: remembered earlier answers, was patient with silence, asked open questions that didn't put words in their mouths, and helped people with unusual career paths describe everyday work as real skill.
  • What it missed: it rarely followed up on emotion, let contradictions pass without comment, weighted every answer the same, and praised everything in the same way.
  • What gave it away: its sentences were too regular, its acknowledgements repeated, and the shifts between sections were audible. The fluency people liked at first was what eventually revealed it as a machine.
"A human would have gone, oh, tell me more about that. The AI asked me its next question about job platforms. That was the moment where I thought: okay, you are processing me, you are not talking with me."

The practical lesson: an AI interview's quality depends less on whether it asks follow-up questions than on what it follows up on.

Do people trust what an AI interviewer concludes about them?

Partly. Trust was calibrated, not all-or-nothing. Respondents trusted the AI to capture their words and spot patterns across many people, and some valued that it doesn't tire or play favourites. They didn't trust it to interpret what their answers meant about them personally, and worried that messy, honest answers would be flattened into tidy conclusions.

"For patterns, yes. For understanding one person, no."

Asked how they'd feel if an AI conversation were used to make a real decision about them, respondents from very different backgrounds named almost the same four conditions:

  • Being told in advance that an AI would be used and how.
  • Being able to see, and correct, what was recorded.
  • Getting a plain-language explanation of the outcome.
  • Knowing which person is accountable for the decision.

Where do people think AI interviews belong?

It depended on the stakes. For screening, first conversations and research at volume, approval was close to unanimous, even among the most critical respondents. For consequential decisions, personal crises and hands-on trades where work has to be seen to be judged, they wanted a human.

One unexpected pattern: people who had often felt judged on their age, accent or career path saw the AI as potentially fairer than a human interviewer. They were also the most insistent on the safeguards above.

"I would prefer it to a manager who does not like my face... But I want to know it is happening and I want somebody to complain to."

How the study was done

The study used a two-stage qualitative design. Each respondent first took part in a short AI-led conversation, then a reflection interview of 19 open questions about the experience, conducted straight afterwards by the researcher. Interviews ran 13 to 40 minutes, were transcribed with Otter.ai, and were analysed with thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke (2006): 31 first-order codes were grouped into 8 second-order themes and 4 aggregate themes.

All sixteen lived in the Netherlands, because the research was carried out during an internship with Heylper, an Amsterdam-based start-up whose product ByAll includes a short AI-supported video conversation.

Limits: a small, purposively chosen sample; one country, one language and one AI system; a single first experience per person; and reflections given to a human researcher. The findings describe these respondents and are offered as a basis for further research, not as a tested theory.
